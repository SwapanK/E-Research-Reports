SLIDE 7 — WHY NOW: THREE CONVERGING FORCES
"The Structural Pillars — Compute · Physical World · Demographics"
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

[INTELLECTUAL, PRECISE — 6 MINUTES]

Three forces are converging simultaneously. Each would be significant in isolation. Together, they create a structural tailwind that distinguishes this AI supercycle from every prior technology wave — including the ones that ended badly.

Force One: Compute Scaling Laws.

The Chinchilla scaling law, established by DeepMind in 2022, demonstrates that AI model capability improves predictably and continuously with additional compute and training data. This is not an opinion — it is an empirically validated relationship observed across six orders of magnitude.

GPT-4 used approximately 10 to the power of 23 floating point operations to train. Next-generation frontier models target 10 to the power of 25 to 26. That is one hundred to one thousand times more compute — and this transition is already underway. Each 10 times increase in compute does not produce incrementally better outputs — it unlocks qualitatively new capabilities. Reasoning. Planning. Physical manipulation.

Here is the structural dynamic: in a competitive market where AI capability determines market position, no single hyperscaler can unilaterally choose to stop scaling. If Microsoft slows investment, Google's models improve relative to Copilot. If Meta stops funding Llama, it falls behind the frontier. The competitive logic locks every major player into an accelerating capital expenditure cycle. This is not a bubble — it is physics in service of competitive necessity.

Force Two: Software Eating the Physical World.

AI models trained in digital simulation can now control physical actuators in the real world. This is called sim-to-real transfer — and it has collapsed the technological barrier between software AI and physical AI.

LiDAR sensors that cost $75,000 in 2012 cost $500 today. NVIDIA's Isaac simulation platform and DeepMind's MuJoCo physics engine allow robot models to accumulate millions of training hours in virtual environments at essentially zero marginal cost. Foundation models built on internet-scale language and image data are now being fine-tuned for robot motion planning. The intelligence developed for software AI transfers — with appropriate adaptation — to physical robots.

The barrier between digital AI and physical AI is collapsing. That has very long-duration investment implications.

Force Three: Demographic and Labour Economics.

Working-age populations are simultaneously shrinking in the United States, the European Union, Japan, and China. Chinese manufacturing wages have risen fivefold since 2005. At a $20,000 total robot cost, a humanoid replaces approximately $35,000 per year in labour — a payback period under two years. Current humanoid robots cost $100,000 to $150,000 — not yet economical at scale. But the cost curve is compressing.

Ageing populations create a separate demand vector: healthcare robotics. Japan — the world's oldest major economy — has the highest robotic density on earth, driven specifically by labour scarcity.

These three forces reinforce each other in a closed loop. Better AI produces better robots. Better robots generate more demand for AI training. More AI training demands more compute. More compute demands more energy and data centre infrastructure. The loop compounds in every direction.

The semiconductor ecosystem is not one industry — it is five distinct sub-industries, each with its own moat structure, its own demand drivers, and its own investment characteristics. Most investors treat it as one. That is an error.

Sub-industry one: EDA Software. Synopsys and Cadence are the duopoly. Every chip ever designed — NVIDIA GPUs, Apple M-series, AMD EPYC — uses one of these tools. Sub-industry two: Materials and Gases. Ultra-pure gases — neon, argon, silane — are required for chip manufacturing at specifications so tight that only incumbents have the qualified supply chains. Air Products, Linde, Showa Denko. The 2022 Ukraine war disrupted neon supplies — Ukraine produced approximately 90% of global semiconductor-grade neon. The industry responded by diversifying, but the vulnerability demonstrated how critical this layer is.

Sub-industry three: Lithography. ASML is the absolute monopoly on EUV — Extreme Ultraviolet lithography. 30 years of accumulated IP in 100,000-plus precision components per machine. No competitor exists. No competitor can be created quickly. ASML produces approximately 60 EUV machines per year. That physical production rate — constrained by the complexity of the machines themselves — is the ultimate bottleneck on the global semiconductor industry's growth rate.

Sub-industry four: Foundry. TSMC, Samsung, Intel Foundry. TSMC's 3-nanometre process has 90-plus-percent market share for the most advanced logic. The yield learning curve on each new process node takes five years — meaning the knowledge required to manufacture reliably at 3 nanometres accumulated over five years of production learning. That cannot be purchased. It can only be earned through production experience.

Sub-industry five: Memory. SK Hynix, Samsung, Micron. HBM — High Bandwidth Memory — is a specialised DRAM product that stacks multiple memory dies vertically and connects them with through-silicon vias. SK Hynix has approximately 50% market share in HBM. The physical integration of HBM with GPU logic via TSMC's CoWoS packaging process is the joint bottleneck that limits how fast NVIDIA can ship Blackwell GPUs.

Now the four player deep-dives: NVIDIA's CUDA ecosystem — 3.5 million developers locked in since 2006. TSMC — process node leadership is a five-year yield learning curve that Intel failed to close. SK Hynix — first to mass-produce HBM3E memory at yields NVIDIA accepts. ASML — the machine that makes the machines, absolute EUV monopoly.

SLIDE 24 — LAYER 4 — HARDWARE SYSTEMS AND NETWORKING
"AI Servers, Training Cluster Architecture, Interconnect Fabric, and Cooling Infrastructure"
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

[TECHNICAL, DETAILED — 6 MINUTES]

This slide covers the physical infrastructure between the GPU and the cloud — the layer most investors skip but which contains critical investment opportunities.

How a training cluster is built — from the GPU level outward.

At the GPU level: 8 H100 or H200 GPUs per server node, interconnected via NVLink at 5.6 terabytes per second. This is the engine room. The GPU is not useful alone — it needs to be connected at high speed to its peers.

At the server node level: GPU plus CPU — typically AMD EPYC — plus HBM memory plus NVMe storage. Form factor ranges from 1U to 10U. Power draw 5 to 10 kilowatts per node.

At the interconnect level: InfiniBand NDR at 400 gigabits per second per GPU enables GPUs across different servers to act as a single unified system. This is what allows a 10,000-GPU cluster to train a single model. NVIDIA owns this layer through its Mellanox acquisition.

At the switching level: top-of-rack switches from Arista, Cisco, or NVIDIA — 100-plus ports at 800 gigabits per second each. Critical for east-west cluster traffic within the data centre.

At the network level: spine and core networking connects racks within the data centre. Ultra-low latency — under one microsecond — is critical for distributed training performance.

Three critical insights on this layer.

The interconnect war: a 10,000-GPU cluster cannot train a model efficiently unless all GPUs can communicate at full bandwidth. InfiniBand (NVIDIA's proprietary solution via Mellanox) versus Ultra-Ethernet (Broadcom coalition) — whoever wins controls the fabric of every AI cluster. Ethernet is winning the cost-per-bandwidth debate. InfiniBand still wins on latency for the most demanding training workloads.

Thermal management is the silent bottleneck: next-generation GPU rack density exceeds 100 kilowatts per rack. Air cooling fails above approximately 20 kilowatts per rack — current technology maximum. Liquid cooling is not optional for 2025-plus deployments. Vertiv, Schneider Electric, and CoolIT are suddenly critical infrastructure companies.

Memory bandwidth, not compute, is the constraint for inference: when serving AI models at scale, the bottleneck is how fast model weights can be loaded from HBM memory into compute units. HBM bandwidth — not raw floating point operations per second — is the AI serving specification that matters most for inference performance.

Key players: NVIDIA Systems sells complete DGX pods — GPU plus InfiniBand plus CUDA as an integrated product. Super Micro Computer designs and assembles AI server nodes. Arista Networks provides cloud networking for data centre spine and leaf architecture. Vertiv Holdings provides thermal management and power distribution for AI servers.

Supply risk: the key technological gap. Until this is solved, humanoids are limited to manipulation tasks with known object shapes and properties.

SLIDE 43 — RISK ANALYSIS: THE BEAR CASES

[BALANCED, ANALYTICAL — 7 MINUTES]

Every strong thesis needs an honest bear case. Let me give you the eight structural risks that could materially impair the AI supercycle thesis — in order of probability and severity.

Risk One: Demand Pull-Forward and Overcapacity. Probability: medium. Severity: high.

The pattern: technology infrastructure booms historically overshoot demand by 18 to 36 months. The telecom bubble of 1999 to 2002 resulted in 10 times more fibre being laid than demand required, with prices collapsing 90%. Could GPU demand be similarly front-loaded?

The case for: hyperscalers are spending $700 billion in 2026 largely to avoid competitive disadvantage — not because every dollar has a clear revenue line attached to it. If AI adoption in enterprise is slower than projected, utilisation rates at cloud providers fall, GPU rental prices collapse, and the infrastructure companies face severe earnings pressure.

The case against: ChatGPT user growth has not slowed. Enterprise AI deployment data from Palantir, Salesforce, and ServiceNow shows accelerating adoption. AI inference demand is growing faster than training demand. Unlike fibre, GPU compute is reconfigurable across different AI tasks — underutilised AI compute finds demand more readily than dark fibre.

Risk Two: China Export Controls and Geopolitical Escalation. Probability: medium-high. Severity: medium.

The case: NVIDIA loses an estimated $15 to $16 billion in annual revenue from H20 export restrictions to China. Escalation to full semiconductor decoupling would eliminate China revenue across the entire semiconductor stack. TSMC in Taiwan represents a concentration risk for the entire global AI industry — no advanced chip manufacturing exists outside of Taiwan and South Korea at scale.

The case against: domestic Chinese AI chip development — Huawei Ascend 910B and others — is improving but still 2 to 3 generations behind NVIDIA. Most TSMC customers have diversified production to Arizona and Japan facilities. The US government's CHIPS Act investments are creating alternative sources.

Risk Three: Custom ASIC Commoditisation. Probability: medium. Severity: medium.

Google TPU v5: comparable performance to H100 for specific inference workloads at lower cost within Google Cloud. AWS Trainium 2: outperforming H100 on specific large language model training benchmarks. Microsoft Maia 100: optimised for OpenAI workload patterns.

The case: hyperscalers collectively could displace 25 to 35% of NVIDIA GPU demand at their own facilities by 2027. If all five hyperscalers achieve this, NVIDIA's addressable market from its top customers shrinks meaningfully.

The case against: custom ASICs are optimised for specific workloads — they cannot replace NVIDIA for novel research, general-purpose AI development, or training new model architectures. The CUDA software moat means researchers insist on NVIDIA even when custom silicon exists. Custom ASICs are an inference story, not a training story. Training still requires NVIDIA.

Risk Four: AI Safety and Regulatory Shock. Probability: low-medium. Severity: extreme.

The EU AI Act: comprehensive AI regulation with fines up to 7% of global revenue. US Senate AI safety hearings increasing frequency. A high-profile AI failure — a self-driving vehicle fatality at scale, a military AI incident, or a major enterprise AI data breach — could trigger emergency regulatory action that pauses deployment.

The case: AI capabilities are advancing faster than regulatory frameworks. A single catastrophic event could trigger legislation that imposes significant costs or restricts commercial deployment.

The case against: regulation will slow not stop the AI buildout. The competitive imperative between US and Chinese AI development limits the severity of regulation the US government is willing to impose.

Risk Five: Open-Source Model Commoditisation. Probability: medium. Severity: high for AI Model layer.

Meta's Llama 4: if open-source model quality reaches closed-model parity — which Llama 3.1 405B is approaching — the revenue model for closed-model API providers like Anthropic and OpenAI collapses. The only moats remaining would be compute infrastructure and enterprise services.

The case: Llama 4 405B is within striking distance of GPT-4o on many benchmarks. DeepSeek R1 demonstrated that Chinese open-source model quality can match frontier closed models. If this trend continues, API pricing goes to zero.

The case against: frontier capability — the very best performance on the most demanding tasks — still requires the enormous compute investments only Microsoft, Google, and OpenAI can sustain. Enterprise trust for compliance and data privacy creates persistent pricing power above the open-source tier.

Risk Six: Energy and Power Constraints. Probability: high. Severity: high.

Data centre power demand is growing faster than the grid can accommodate. Grid connection queues in Virginia — data centre alley — run 5 to 7 years. New nuclear plants take 10 to 15 years to build. Renewable energy is intermittent and cannot provide the firm baseload AI data centres require.

The case: AI scaling laws require 100 times more compute over the next 5 years. The grid cannot keep pace. This becomes the binding constraint on AI capability growth — not chips or capital, but electrons.

The case against: Small modular reactors, gas turbine additions, and grid upgrades are all accelerating. Hyperscalers are signing 20-year power purchase agreements precisely to secure supply. The financial incentive to solve this problem is enormous.

Risk Seven: Robotics Timeline Overestimation. Probability: high. Severity: medium.

The base rate for robotics timelines: every robotic programme in history has taken 18 to 24 months longer than management projected. The case against: the AI foundation model convergence with robotics is a genuine step-change. The rate of robot capability improvement in 2024 to 2026 — as measured by dexterity, task generalisation, and sim-to-real transfer — is materially faster than prior robotics development curves.

[INTELLECTUAL, FORWARD-LOOKING — 5 MINUTES]

Small modular reactors — SMRs — represent the most transformative long-term option in the AI power stack. They are also the highest-risk and longest-duration play. Let me be precise about both dimensions.

The technology concept: rather than building the largest nuclear plant possible — which takes 10 to 15 years and costs $15 to $25 billion — SMRs aim to build reactors that can be factory-manufactured and deployed on-site in months. Target capacity: 10 to 300 megawatts per unit, modular and stackable. Target cost: dramatically lower than conventional nuclear through manufacturing learning curve effects.

The vision for AI: a hyperscaler deploys an SMR directly on its data centre campus. No grid connection. No power purchase agreement. Firm, 24/7, zero-carbon power directly powering the data centre. Data centre power independence, eliminating the 5 to 7 year grid connection queue.

The reality in 2026: no commercial SMR has operated anywhere in the world. The technology is proven — naval nuclear reactors have operated for decades on this scale — but commercial licensing, siting, and construction are all untested at speed. Every SMR company's timeline has slipped.

Oklo Inc — OKLO. The most advanced Western SMR startup.

The milestone: first Nuclear Regulatory Commission combined licence application submitted. This is the most advanced NRC application from any advanced fission company in the US. Not approved yet — but submitted.

Timeline: first commercial Aurora unit targeted for 2027 to 2028. This is the most aggressive commercial schedule in the SMR sector — and it requires flawless NRC approval and construction execution.

Risk: pre-revenue. NRC approval is not guaranteed. Construction delays are the historical norm, not the exception, in nuclear. Sam Altman's co-founder role creates both attention and distraction risk.

NuScale Power — SMR. The first NRC-approved SMR design in US history.

The technology: VOYGR plant design using small pressurised water reactors — the same proven technology as conventional large nuclear, scaled down. 77 megawatts per module, up to 12 modules per plant.

The milestone: NRC design certification approved in January 2023 — the first and only SMR design to receive NRC approval in the US. This is a genuine competitive advantage.

Challenge: the Carbon Free Power Project in Idaho — a 6-module, 462-megawatt plant contracted with 23 Utah municipalities — was cancelled in November 2023 when cost estimates rose from $5.3 billion to $9.3 billion, exceeding what municipalities would pay. This was a significant setback demonstrating that SMR cost projections have not yet been validated at construction scale.

Verdict on SMR broadly: the technology is real. The regulatory frameworks are being built. The first commercial deployments are likely to occur in the 2028 to 2032 timeframe. For investors with appropriate time horizons and risk tolerance, Oklo and the SMR sector represent the most important long-term power infrastructure bet in the AI stack. For near-term focused investors, Constellation Energy and GE Vernova provide the same AI power theme with real current revenue and cash flow.
